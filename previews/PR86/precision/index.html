How precise is a p-value?

A p-value from trials trials estimates the exact permutation p-value (every within-block arrangement counted), and more trials sharpen the estimate without changing the data. MonteCarloPValue holds a p-value together with the number of trials it came from, and hdi and probability_below describe its Monte Carlo error. The result of solve is unchanged; build the type from a result's pvalue and trials, or from the counts.

using Random, RandomizedTukeyHSD

responses = [0.10 0.40 0.70
             0.20 0.50 0.60
             0.15 0.45 0.80
             0.25 0.35 0.65]
problem = RandomizedTukeyProblem(responses)
results = solve(problem, FixedTrials(10_000); rng=Xoshiro(1))
p = MonteCarloPValue(results[2].pvalue, 10_000)
MonteCarloPValue(37, 10000)
pvalue(p)                         # the p-value, exceeding / trials
0.0037
hdi(p)                            # (lower, upper) for the exact p-value
(0.0026316350011594196, 0.005023339217247846)
hdi(p; level=0.9)
(0.002787050734864183, 0.004792595282603118)
hdi(p; prior=(0.5, 0.5))          # Jeffreys' prior instead of the uniform
(0.002589906707669584, 0.004965859932406946)
probability_below(p, 0.05)        # probability the exact p-value < 0.05
1.0
MonteCarloPValue(49, 1000)        # from the counts: 49 of 1000 trials
MonteCarloPValue(49, 1000)
  • The trials that reached a pair's difference are binomial in the exact p-value. A prior=(a, b) is a Beta(a, b) distribution for it, uniform by default, and its distribution afterwards is a Beta distribution too. hdi is that distribution's highest density interval: the shortest one holding level (0.95 by default) of the probability. Read it as "given these trials and the prior, the exact p-value is in here with 95% probability".
  • It is a Bayesian credible interval, not a confidence interval, and has no coverage guarantee. The prior is a choice and matters only for small counts; at zero exceedances the upper end is 3.0 / trials for the uniform prior and 1.9 / trials for Jeffreys'. Only Beta priors are supported.
  • A p-value of 0 gives an interval from 0 to about 3 / trials: the trials rule out little more than that.
  • probability_below(p, alpha) is the chance that the exact p-value is below alpha, which shows whether more trials could put the p-value on the other side of it. For an observed 0.049 it is 0.52 at 1000 trials, 0.67 at 10 000 and 0.93 at 100 000. The library takes no alpha of its own; the decision is yours.
  • The interval covers the Monte Carlo error only: not the assumptions of the test, the floating-point ties (Known limitation: floating-point ties), or that many pairs are tested (the range statistic already accounts for that). The intervals of different pairs are not simultaneous.
  • Decide trials and the seed before looking at the results. Running again until a p-value falls below a level, or until its interval does, is not a valid procedure; the interval does not make it one.
  • pvalue is the generic function of StatsAPI, which the Julia statistics packages share, so it is the same function after using HypothesisTests. hdi is not confint, which is a confidence interval.

Choosing the number of trials

trials_for turns a precision target into a number of trials, for FixedTrials. It is decided before the run, from the precision wanted, so it does not depend on the results.

julia> trials_for(StandardError(0.001))
250000

julia> trials_for(StandardError(0.001); p=0.05)
47500

julia> trials_for(HDIWidth(0.002); p=0.05)
182473
trials_for(HDIWidth(0.002; level=0.9, prior=(0.5, 0.5)); p=0.05)
128509

Then solve(problem, FixedTrials(trials_for(HDIWidth(0.002); p=0.05))).

  • StandardError(se): the standard error of a p-value is at most se. It is sqrt(p * (1 - p) / trials), the standard deviation of a binomial proportion, which holds without assuming a normal distribution.
  • HDIWidth(width; level=0.95, prior=(1, 1)): the hdi of a p-value is at most width wide, for the same level and prior as hdi. It assumes the count that p implies. For many trials it agrees with StandardError at width / 3.92 (95% level); they differ in what they say.
  • The precision depends on the exact p-value, which is not known before the run. p=0.5 (the default) is the worst case and meets the target for every pair; a p near the level you will judge pairs at, like 0.05, needs fewer trials, and meets the target for p-values near it.
  • A pilot run can suggest p, but its trials are not part of the final run.
  • A new kind of target is a type with a method trials_for(target, p).
