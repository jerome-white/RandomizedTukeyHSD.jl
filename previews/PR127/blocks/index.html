What counts as a block

Each trial shuffles the responses of every block independently of every other block. So the test treats the blocks as independent units, each sampled, or randomized, on its own. When several rows of your data come from one such unit, aggregate them to one response for the unit before you build the problem. Otherwise the shuffle splits values that belong together, the shuffled ranges vary less than they should, and the p-values are too small.

Two cases are common: one treatment measured several times in one block, and blocks that were sampled together. They call for different aggregations.

Repeated responses

Here each treatment has several responses in one block: a language model answering one question K times, a lab measuring one sample in replicate, a rater scoring one item twice. The tidy table then has K rows for the block and treatment, and the library rejects it with an error that names the pair.

Make the response the mean of the K values. The test takes fractional responses unchanged, for example the fraction of K answers that were correct. The mean, and not the sum, is the right summary when K differs between blocks or treatments, because a sum would favor whichever treatment was measured more often.

using DataFrames, Statistics

df = combine(
    groupby(df, [:block, :treatment]),
    :response => mean => :response,
)

Giving each repeat its own block id (q1#1, q1#2, ...) gets past the error, but it is wrong. It makes K correlated values K blocks, which the shuffle sends to different treatments independently. Miller (2024, Section 3.1) makes the same point for standard errors.

The repeats must be independent draws under the same conditions: for a language model, a fresh conversation for every answer, with the same settings. Repeats that share state, such as answers within one conversation, vary less than independent ones, and their mean is less precise than it looks.

The library rejects the duplicate rows instead of averaging them for you, because the mean is one choice among several (the median, a majority vote, the best of K), and which one answers your question is your decision, as with Missing responses.

Blocks sampled together

Here blocks come in groups that were sampled as a unit, and the responses of a treatment are correlated within a group: several questions about one passage, one problem translated into several languages, patients of one clinic, plots in one field. A model that misreads a passage tends to miss all of its questions; a clinic that runs a treatment poorly does so for all of its patients. Call such a group a cluster.

Make the cluster the block. Its response for a treatment is the sum of that treatment's responses over the cluster's blocks, divided by the average number of blocks in a cluster:

using DataFrames

average = length(unique(df.block)) / length(unique(df.cluster))
df = combine(
    groupby(df, [:cluster, :treatment]),
    :response => (r -> sum(r) / average) => :response,
)
problem = RandomizedTukeyProblem(
    df;
    block=:cluster,
    treatment=:treatment,
    response=:response,
)

This is exactly the test that gives every block of a cluster the same arrangement of treatments, with clusters arranged independently. With n blocks in C clusters, and S_c[t] the sum of treatment t's responses over cluster c, a trial that gives cluster c the arrangement p_c makes treatment t's mean

(1/n) * sum over c of S_c[p_c(t)]

which is the mean the test computes from one row per cluster holding S_c / (n/C). So the p-values are those of the clustered shuffle, and the means and differences in the results are those of the original blocks. Dividing by n/C changes nothing else: it scales the observed differences and every shuffled range alike.

With clusters of equal size, this response is the mean of the cluster's blocks. With unequal sizes, the plain mean of each cluster is a different comparison: it weights every cluster equally, where the sum weights every block equally. Either can be the question you want answered, but only the sum reproduces the clustered shuffle.

The aggregation needs every block in one cluster and, as always, a response for every treatment in every block.

Few clusters, coarse p-values

After aggregating there are only C blocks, and with m treatments no exact p-value can be below (m!)^(1 - C). Relabeling the treatments in the same way in every cluster leaves the range unchanged, so at least m! of the (m!)^C arrangements reach the observed range. With 3 treatments in 4 clusters that floor is 1/216; with 3 treatments in 2 clusters it is 1/6. This is the price of the dependence in the data. More trials do not lower it, only more clusters do.

Clusters and strata

Whether to aggregate follows from how the blocks were sampled, not from correlation alone (Abadie et al., 2023). Aggregate when the blocks were drawn in clusters from a larger population you mean to generalize to. Groups fixed by the design, such as the subjects of a benchmark chosen to cover a list, are strata. Every stratum is present by design, and its blocks can stay as they are.
